V. EXPERIMENT DESIGN
To systematically investigate our research questions on
gesture realization and recognition across morphologies, we
evaluate a broad set of 50 gesture cues across five real robot
embodiments and three synthetic morphologies.

B. Metrics
1) Human Recognition: We use a hard-negative 4-
Alternative Forced-Choice task (4AFC; 25% chance) to test
whether an observer can recover the intended cue from
a silent motion clip. We select the three nearest cues in
the Motion-Program representation using Jaccard similarity.
These alternatives are fixed across participants, while their
presentation order is randomized. Participants watch silent
clips without a viewing-time and replay limit before selection
considering the cognitive load. Ten participants each evaluate
50 unique trials, with no trial repeated across participants.
2) Attribute-Level Failure Analysis: To understand why a
generated gesture succeeds or fails, we additionally compare
its Location, Orientation, and Movement attributes against
the corresponding human-authored ground truth (GT).

the relevant carrier (end effector), normalized to the
robot’s reachable range (e.g., z ∈ [0, 100], where 0 and

• Orientation, Movement: We evaluate these attributes
using discrete exact matches. Reach uses six possible
directions—front, back, up, down, in, and out—giving

(x/y/z; 1/3) and sign (+/−/±; 1/3).
3) Cross-Morphology Realization Analysis: For non-
humanoid morphologies, we operationalize the realization
strategies introduced in §III-C according to how the cue-
relevant carrier and attributes change across morphologies.
• Preserved: The same or anatomically corresponding
carrier is available, and its recognition-relevant Move-
ment, Orientation, and Location are approximately re-
tained.
• Carrier-transferred: The original carrier is unavail-
able, but another carrier reproduces the characteristic
Movement while maintaining an appropriate Orientation
and Location. If the movement is transferred but its
orientation or location is inconsistent with the cue, we
label it a Transfer Error.
• Rematerialized: The original carrier is unavailable
and the characteristic Movement changes, but the new
movement, orientation, and location together provide
an appropriate realization of the intended cue. If the
changed movement does not appropriately reconstruct
the remaining attributes, or if the recognition-relevant
movement disappears altogether, we label it a Remate-
rialization Error.
VI. RESULTS
A. Human-like Robots
a) Human recognition: The 4AFC study shows a clear
gap between human- and LLM-authored programs. Human-
authored programs are recognized at 96.7% on G1 and 93.3%
on TIAGo, whereas Gemini-authored programs reach only
63.3% and 60.0%, respectively. Performance changes little
between G1 and TIAGo within each authoring condition.

but persists across two broadly human-like bodies despite

b) Attribute-level errors: Table ?? localizes this gap at
the attribute level. For location, Gemini’s mean z error is 11.2
body-% on G1 and 8.7 on TIAGo, while errors are larger
along x and y (18.4/19.2 on G1 and 14.7/19.8 on TIAGo).
Categorical attributes show a different pattern. Reach agree-
ment is 47.2% and 49.1%, well above its 1/6 chance level,
indicating that the model often selects a broadly appropriate
direction. Palm orientation is much weaker: Gemini reaches
48.6% on G1 and 52.8% on TIAGo, compared with chance
levels of 25% and 50%, respectively. This suggests that
palm configuration is not reliably preserved, particularly
when it is not strongly constrained by the cue. Movement
is more accurately captured overall, with axis agreement
of 58.0% and 63.2% and sign agreement of 56.8% and
72.2%. However, the remaining errors are often semantically
consequential: cues such as air quotes can activate the
appropriate carrier and repeated motion while moving along
an incorrect axis.
c) Qualitative patterns.: The LLM’s prior knowledge
is most useful for cues with a conventional iconic form.
For depict flying bird, it spreads both arms, turns the
palms down, and reproduces the GT’s four z-axis flaps; for
depict big bowl, it places both palms up and traces a bi-
lateral yz arc. It also selects the expected carrier without con-
fusion for movement-dominant cues: nod yes uses head tilt,
shake head no uses head pan, and gaze shift avoid
keeps the GT’s head-pan carrier. The failures instead reveal
semantic completion without geometric grounding: because
generation is single-pass and does not inspect the rendered
body, the model often captures the verbal concept but assigns
attributes that do not realize it physically. In point self,
for example, the GT points back toward the torso, whereas
the LLM sets dir=front; in cover yawn sleepy, it
moves a hand toward the face but replaces the GT’s upward,
horizontal hand with an inward, vertical palm. Likewise,
its bowl has the correct palm-up arc but reverses the GT’s
outward reach to inward. Thus, semantic knowledge reliably
proposes what should move and the rough iconic stroke,
while embodied orientation remains the principal grounding
failure.
B. Non-anthropomorphic Robots
a) Human recognition: Figure 4 shows that unfamiliar
morphology does not itself prevent communication. Human-
authored programs remain recognizable on IIWA (90.0%),
Stretch (93.3%), and Roomba (90.0%), despite the progres-
sive loss of hands, a head, and articulated limbs. LLM-
authored programs instead decline from 60.0% to 56.7% and
46.7%, widening the human–LLM gap from 30.0 to 43.3
percentage points.
b) Cue-generation richness.: In Table ??, program
length counts components such as pose, move, path
while Attributes/program sums specified attributes across
components in a program. Length alone, however, can-
not distinguish geometrically different motions. We there-
fore introduce Types/Cue: the number of distinct dynamic
families—oscillation, line, arc, and one-way joint motion.
IIWA is similar under both authoring methods, but with
Stretch, Human-authored programs are longer (3.9 vs. 2.8),
contain more attributes (15.3 vs. 10.7), and combine more
motion families (1.33 vs. 1.10). On Roomba, the LLM
is longer (3.2 vs. 2.8) yet uses fewer attributes (9.1 vs.
11.2) and fewer families (1.20 vs. 1.57). Thus, the LLM
often adds steps without adding a comparably rich motion
vocabulary, whereas humans vary geometry, timing, and
carrier combinations according to the cue.
c) Morphological collapse on Roomba.: This lack of
compositional diversity helps explain why Roomba clips
are especially confusable with their hard negatives. Across
the complete 50-cue LLM set, 34/50 programs (68.0%)
use fore–aft translation. Of those, 21/34 (61.8%) contain
no additional rotation, arc, or lateral motion. Once the
body has collapsed to a single mobile base, such programs
map semantically different cues—including point you,
salute, highfive invite, and stretch arms up—
to variants of approach, retreat, and hold, reducing their
contrast against nearby answer choices. Human authors in-
stead use the base’s orientation and trajectory as surrogate
anatomy. In beckon come here, they retreat, rotate 90◦
so the elongated body reads like an extended carrier, and
then oscillate its heading. These constructions do not restore
the missing limbs, but they approximate the intended spatial
relation with a richer sequence of planar evidence, consistent
with the higher Human Types/Cue value.
d) Qualitative grounding.: The contrast between
yield turn and request turn, shows how the same
semantic distinction changes with the available morphology.
On IIWA, both programs place a front-facing horizontal end
effector near the body center (y=50); yield turn starts at
x=65, z=50 and traces a downward xz arc (−90◦

C. New Morphologies
We use synthetic morphologies to ask two questions: (1)
Can recognition-relevant attributes be defined for complex
bodies far from human? and (2) Can these attributes be
composed into Motion Programs that convey intended mean-
ings through such unfamiliar bodies to LLM? Five familiar
platforms may benefit from memorized body-specific map-
pings. We therefore introduce three synthetic morphologies
as an out-of-distribution probe that removes this shortcut.
As shown in Figure 5, TrilegCrown has no head, hands, or
stable front; SpineCrab replaces conventional limbs with a
canted spine and two unequal pincers; and PaddleClaw, the
least unfamiliar of the three, retains a head but replaces the
upper body with a paddle and a claw.
a) Unfamiliar parts become communicative carriers:
The programs do not route every cue through a single
surrogate limb. On TrilegCrown, 20 of the 30 cues use
the front leg, 17 the base, and 9 and 7 the two other
legs; on SpineCrab, 22 use the long pincer, 12 the short
pincer, and 11 the spine. Carrier choice follows the cue:
depict flying bird oscillates TrilegCrown’s two rear
legs, shake head no twists SpineCrab’s spine in place,
and tilt head curious turns SpineCrab aside with its
antenna pointed forward.
b) The same meaning is rematerialized, not only reas-
signed: For 15 of the 30 cues, the motion structure itself—
static pose, joint oscillation, or traced path—differs across
the three bodies, so the change goes beyond swapping a car-
rier name. nod yes is a head tilt on PaddleClaw and a spine
pitch on SpineCrab, but an in-place yaw rock of the base on
TrilegCrown. beckon come here is a repeated claw or
pincer-tip oscillation on the two branched bodies, whereas
TrilegCrown raises its front leg, curls the knee three times,
and advances toward the viewer. depict big bowl traces
a vertical end-effector arc on PaddleClaw and SpineCrab
but a 180◦
floor arc of the whole base on TrilegCrown,
changing carrier, plane, and scale together. These cases are
the morphology-conditioned rematerialization of Sec. III-C,
not anatomical substitution.
c) Where the probe breaks: The same programs expose
three failures. Contrast collapse: as channels disappear, dis-
tinct cues share one program. On TrilegCrown, point you,
salute, and flex bicep all reduce to the same presented
front leg, and nod yes and shake head no both become
base yaw oscillations that differ only in amplitude (±12◦
vs.
±25◦
), erasing the vertical–lateral axis contrast that defines
them (Sec. III-B). On SpineCrab, cover yawn sleepy
and facepalm are identical, and on PaddleClaw five cues
are static claw poses distinguished only by a direction
token. Human-form leakage: six bilateral cues, including
arms open welcome, cross arms defensive, and
depict flying bird, set SpineCrab’s short pincer out
or in, a configuration its schema does not expose, in effect
reconstructing a human pair of arms; TrilegCrown’s front leg
likewise stands in for a hand across pointing, saluting, and
flexing.

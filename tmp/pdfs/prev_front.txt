Beyond Imitation: Recognizable Gestures Across Robot Morphologies
Anonymous Authors
Abstract— Humans readily infer intent and social meaning
from motion, even when the moving body is far from human
form. Robots are subject to the same perceptual process,
as recognizable gestures do not require a human-like body,
but do require motion that provides clear evidence for the
intended meaning. We characterize this evidence through three
attributes—orientation, body-relative location, and movement—
and introduce Symbolic Motion Programs (Motion Programs),
an executable symbolic representation that makes these at-
tributes explicit across different robot morphologies. We evalu-
ate 50 gesture cues across eight morphologies, including human-
like, non-anthropomorphic, and novel synthetic robots. On
human-like G1 and TIAGo, human-authored programs achieve
96.7% and 93.3% recognition, compared with 63.3% and 60.0%
for LLM-authored programs. Although LLMs often select the
appropriate body part, they frequently mis-specify its orienta-
tion, location, or movement. On non-anthropomorphic IIWA,
Stretch, and Roomba, familiar human body parts disappear,
requiring the same meaning to be expressed through different
parts or motions. Failures here expose a form of imitation
without sufficient understanding: models can reproduce familiar
human movements, yet struggle to determine what made them
meaningful and express that meaning through a different body.
Finally, on three novel synthetic morphologies, LLMs make
use of unfamiliar body parts and construct new realizations
of gestures, but different meanings can collapse into similar
motions with non-anthropomorphic cues.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humans capacity to see beyond the physical trajectory of
an action and infer inner semantics has been fundamental to
how we communicate and coordinate with one another [1].
Remarkably, our innate sensitivity is not confined to the
human body: we readily recover animate structure from only
a handful of moving points [2], infer goals and intentions
from the actions of other agents [3], and perceive agency
and social interaction even in the motion of simple geometric
shapes [4]. As robots increasingly coexist in human environ-
ments, we argue that they inevitably enter this same percep-
tual space — Every trajectory is also available for human
interpretation conveying what the robot intends, attends to,
or will do next [5]. From this perspective, communicative
motion is not decorative behavior layered on top of robot
action, but an intrinsic dimension of the every action itself.
Prior work on expressive motion for non-anthropomorphic
robots has largely focused on latent properties of behavior,
such as personality, affect, or mood. These properties are well
suited to cross-embodiment expression, as they can often
be conveyed by modulating general characteristics such as
speed, amplitude, rhythm, or smoothness. Humans readily
perceive differences along these familiar latent properties.

In contrast, gestures require compositional realizations. A
gesture such as stop, beckon, or nod yes depends not
only on how a robot moves, but on whether particular spatial
and directional relationships are realized. Consequently, prior
work on such gestures has often relied on human-authored
behaviors or imported human motion for a small number of
robot platforms and morphologies.
To express a gesture, humanoids have intuitive and high-
resolution carriers in their movement, enabled by many
degrees of freedom. We posit that three attributes are most
commonly important for producing a recognizable cue—
orientation, location, and movement. As illustrated in Fig-
ure 1, ‘beckon’ requires the hand to occupy an appropriate
location, face a particular direction, and repeatedly move
inward toward the body. For morphologies such as a mobile
manipulator, a single arm, or a Roomba-like base, these
correspondences become different or disappear entirely. The
problem is therefore no longer simply to reproduce the same
configuration or movement, but to determine which evidence
should be preserved and which available part of the robot can
carry it.
We use Symbolic Motion Programs to make this problem
explicit and to evaluate how well current LLMs can perform
this translation. A Motion Program can be authored by
either a human or an LLM, inspected and edited at the

Our results show that on humanoids, errors often arise
from insufficient precision in recognition-critical attributes
such as orientation or location. On more constrained bodies,
LLMs frequently reproduce superficial aspects of a demon-
strated motion without preserving the evidence that made
the original gesture recognizable. In Figure 1, oscillating
a Roomba-like base may superficially imitate the repetitive
motion of pat stomach or beckon, yet leave the intended
meaning highly ambiguous. Human-authored motions, in
contrast, often combine multiple aspects of the available
motion to convey the intended cue: for beckon, the base
first turns and then makes short inward movements, while
for depict flying bird, the absence of arms is com-
pensated for by a broader bird-like path of the base. Our
contributions are:
1) A perceptual formulation of cross-embodiment ges-
ture: discrete meanings are described through orienta-
tion, body-relative location, and movement.
2) An executable, editable generation framework: Mo-
tion Programs let humans and LLMs author the same
pose/movement/path representation and compile it
deterministically across bodies.
3) A diagnosis of present limits: humanoid experiments
and constrained-body cases show where generated ges-
tures preserve, rematerialize, augment, distort, or lose
communicative evidence.

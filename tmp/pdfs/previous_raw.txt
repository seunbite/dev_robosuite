Beyond Imitation: Recognizable Gestures Across Robot Morphologies
Anonymous Authors
Abstract— Humans readily infer intent and social meaning
from motion, even when the moving body is far from human
form. Robots are subject to the same perceptual process,
as recognizable gestures do not require a human-like body,
but do require motion that provides clear evidence for the
intended meaning. We characterize this evidence through three
attributes—orientation, body-relative location, and movement—
and introduce Symbolic Motion Programs (Motion Programs),
an executable symbolic representation that makes these at-
tributes explicit across different robot morphologies. We evalu-
ate 50 gesture cues across eight morphologies, including human-
like, non-anthropomorphic, and novel synthetic robots. On
human-like G1 and TIAGo, human-authored programs achieve
96.7% and 93.3% recognition, compared with 63.3% and 60.0%
for LLM-authored programs. Although LLMs often select the
appropriate body part, they frequently mis-specify its orienta-
tion, location, or movement. On non-anthropomorphic IIWA,
Stretch, and Roomba, familiar human body parts disappear,
requiring the same meaning to be expressed through different
parts or motions. Failures here expose a form of imitation
without sufficient understanding: models can reproduce familiar
human movements, yet struggle to determine what made them
meaningful and express that meaning through a different body.
Finally, on three novel synthetic morphologies, LLMs make
use of unfamiliar body parts and construct new realizations
of gestures, but different meanings can collapse into similar
motions with non-anthropomorphic cues.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humans capacity to see beyond the physical trajectory of
an action and infer inner semantics has been fundamental to
how we communicate and coordinate with one another [1].
Remarkably, our innate sensitivity is not confined to the
human body: we readily recover animate structure from only
a handful of moving points [2], infer goals and intentions
from the actions of other agents [3], and perceive agency
and social interaction even in the motion of simple geometric
shapes [4]. As robots increasingly coexist in human environ-
ments, we argue that they inevitably enter this same percep-
tual space — Every trajectory is also available for human
interpretation conveying what the robot intends, attends to,
or will do next [5]. From this perspective, communicative
motion is not decorative behavior layered on top of robot
action, but an intrinsic dimension of the every action itself.
Prior work on expressive motion for non-anthropomorphic
robots has largely focused on latent properties of behavior,
such as personality, affect, or mood. These properties are well
suited to cross-embodiment expression, as they can often
be conveyed by modulating general characteristics such as
speed, amplitude, rhythm, or smoothness. Humans readily
perceive differences along these familiar latent properties.

In contrast, gestures require compositional realizations. A
gesture such as stop, beckon, or nod yes depends not
only on how a robot moves, but on whether particular spatial
and directional relationships are realized. Consequently, prior
work on such gestures has often relied on human-authored
behaviors or imported human motion for a small number of
robot platforms and morphologies.
To express a gesture, humanoids have intuitive and high-
resolution carriers in their movement, enabled by many
degrees of freedom. We posit that three attributes are most
commonly important for producing a recognizable cue—
orientation, location, and movement. As illustrated in Fig-
ure 1, ‘beckon’ requires the hand to occupy an appropriate
location, face a particular direction, and repeatedly move
inward toward the body. For morphologies such as a mobile
manipulator, a single arm, or a Roomba-like base, these
correspondences become different or disappear entirely. The
problem is therefore no longer simply to reproduce the same
configuration or movement, but to determine which evidence
should be preserved and which available part of the robot can
carry it.
We use Symbolic Motion Programs to make this problem
explicit and to evaluate how well current LLMs can perform
this translation. A Motion Program can be authored by
either a human or an LLM, inspected and edited at the

contributions are:
1) A perceptual formulation of cross-embodiment ges-

tion, body-relative location, and movement.
2) An executable, editable generation framework: Mo-
tion Programs let humans and LLMs author the same
pose/movement/path representation and compile it
deterministically across bodies.
3) A diagnosis of present limits: humanoid experiments
and constrained-body cases show where generated ges-
tures preserve, rematerialize, augment, distort, or lose
communicative evidence.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Expressive Robot Motion Generation
We posit human-like robot body not a prerequisite to
express concrete mind state in daily life. Early work on
communicative robot motion authored robot-specific ges-
tures through multimodal behavior languages [6], [7], [8] or
designed learning to reproduce human motion from paired
speech and motion data [9], [10], [11] using humanoid body.
More recent work has shown that communicative motion
on a human-like body planting timing, energy, and Laban-
derived motion qualities to shape confidence, affect, and
social state [12], [13], [14]. These studies establish that
non-humanoid motion can be socially legible, but typically
focus on latent affective dimensions or platform-specific
repertoires. On the other hand, we study how a concrete
communicative meaning is realized as the available body
parts and motion channels change across morphologies.
B. Motion Generation with Large Language Models (LLMs)
Language models generate robot motion at several levels:
they can emit code that invokes skills [15], construct spatial
objectives or value maps that guide control [16], select or pa-
rameterize learned motion primitives [17], and generate or re-
trieve expressive animations from a prompted repertoire [18].
Fig. 2. We characterize gestures across morphologies through family resem-
blance: attributes such as Orientation, Location, and Movement contribute
with varying importance to the recognition of different gesture cues.
Across these systems, reliability is sought through structured
APIs, few-shot demonstrations, executable constraints, ob-
jective construction, or verification and correction around
the model output. Most optimize task completion or assume
a predefined skill library. For gesture, however, physical
execution is not the endpoint: the motion succeeds only when
an observer recovers its intended meaning (recognition). Our
distinction is therefore representational that Motion Programs
expose recognition-critical attributes as editable primitives,
allowing the same automatic authoring pipeline to be retained
across robots while the renderer changes how those attributes
are physically realized.
III. GESTURE GENERATION FRAMEWORK
A. Gesture: Meaning and Action Mapping
Gesture generation maps a meaning space G (e.g., nodding
yes, stopping, beckoning, or depicting a flying bird) to
an action space A(M) shaped by robot morphology M.
For example, a Roomba realizes motion primarily through
changes in x, y, and θ, whereas a humanoid has many
articulated carriers. Within each action space, a meaning
can admit infinitely many continuous realizations, but not
all variations contribute equally to recognition. We suggest
that some attributes are recognition-critical: changing them
can weaken the evidence for the intended cue G and shift
interpretation toward plausible alternatives G′
. For example,
reversing the palm orientation in a stop gesture can make
the motion resemble ‘looking into a hand mirror’.
B. Recognition-Critical Attributes
In Figure 2, we identify three important attributes that
commonly contribute to gesture recognition, with their rela-
tive importance varying across gesture cues. This formulation
follows the concept of family resemblance [19], in which cat-
egory members share overlapping features without requiring
a single set of necessary and sufficient conditions, making
some members more typical than others (e.g., typ(robin |
bird) > typ(penguin | bird)).

a) Orientation (O): Orientation describes where a
recognition-relevant carrier—such as the head, hand, foot,
or torso—is directed. Although orientation is physically
continuous, we argue that many communicative distinctions
can be captured by a small set of discrete relations: front,
back, in, out, up, and down. For example, point you
should orient its pointing carrier toward the addressee in
front; stop palm out presents the palm toward front while
directing the fingertips up.
b) Location (L): Location describes where the relevant
carrier lies relative to the body or environment. Location is
naturally continuous and can require different spatial reso-
lutions across cues, for example, in rub eye an incorrect
z coordinate can move the end effector from the eye to
the forehead or nose, changing the intended interpretation.
Therefore, continuous (x, y, z) locations are needed to spec-
ify precise cue realization.
c) Movement (M): Movement describes how a carrier
changes over time and captures temporal structure that
cannot be specified by pose alone. Movement differs in
axis/sign (movement direction), extent, speed, and repetition.
Among these, axis and sign often provide particularly strong
recognition evidence. A nod requires repeated motion along
the vertical (z) axis, whereas a head shake requires lateral
(y) oscillation. Similarly, raise fists requires upward
(z+) movement rather than downward (z−) movement.

d) Realization strategy: Transferring a gesture to a
different morphology changes which carriers and attributes
are physically available. Movement structure is often com-
paratively portable: an axis, direction, or repetition pattern
can be reconstructed with other body parts in the changed
morphology. Orientation may instead need to migrate to
another carrier when the original one does not exist, while
location is particularly morphology-dependent because body-
relative landmarks and reachable regions change with em-
bodiment.
• Preserved: The original or anatomically corresponding
carrier remains available, and the recognition-relevant
movement and pose attributes are retained (e.g., a
human head nod → Tiago head nod).
• Carrier-transferred: The original carrier is unavail-
able, but another carrier reproduces approximately the
same recognition-relevant attributes (e.g., vertical head
oscillation → vertical single-arm oscillation).
• Rematerialized: The target morphology requires the
communicative structure itself to be reformulated,
changing movement, orientation, or spatial relations
rather than simply assigning them to another carrier
(e.g., bilateral arm flapping → a bird-like trajectory
traced by a planar mobile robot).
IV. SYMBOLIC MOTION PROGRAMS
We introduce Symbolic Motion Programs (Motion Pro-
grams), a compact executable representation of the evidence
that makes a gesture recognizable. As illustrated in Fig. 3, a
program composes three typed primitives: pose specifies a
carrier’s orientation and body-relative location; movement
applies a joint-level change to an articulated carrier; and
path traces a Cartesian trajectory with an end effector or
mobile base. For example, the human-authored G1 program
for beckon come here is

z=80, x=60) + move(wrist, x, [-35,+35],
rep=3, spd=3.5, hold=0.1)

A. Pre-authoring Stage: Design by Human Authors
1) Morphology Schema (YAML): To provide both human
and LLM authors with explicit information about each
robot’s available motion channels, we encode the morphol-
ogy as a text, YAML schema. The schema specifies the
meaning, value range (discrete or continuous), default value,
and examples for each available attribute. The grammar
of movement and path remains largely shared across
embodiments, using attributes such as joint, axis, angle,
speed, repetition, hold time, and arc and line parameters.
In contrast, pose attributes are more morphology-specific
and act as filters over the robot’s available pose set to get
the appropriate configuration.
2) Few-Shot Examples: Human authors manually de-
sign the same five cue—raising hand greeting, wave hi,
bow greeting, blow kiss, and circle temple crazy—for each
morphology. These cues do not overlap with the 50 cues
used in our evaluation. Given only these examples and the
morphology’s YAML, few shot generation can be automated
with an LLM authoring and human inspection.
3) Feasible Pose Sets: We additionally construct fea-
sible pose sets for G1 and TIAGo. Both have human-
like upper bodies, their joint ranges exceed typical hu-
man articulation; therefore physically valid but unnaturally
folded pose hampers gesture recognizability. We found this
difficult to filter reliably with a VLM, and instead use a
deterministic procedure. We enumerate joint configurations
over −90◦

within each robot’s limits and retain
poses that (1) are collision-free, (2) place the end effector

45.2% of candidate poses for G1 and 93.6% for TIAGo.
B. Automated Stage: LLM Authoring and Simulation
a) Program authoring.: For each cue–morphology pair,
the LLM receives the cue name, YAML description of the
target morphology, and five shots rather than the raw XML.
The model then produces a single Motion Program. A deter-
ministic parser converts the generated text into an executable
configuration and performs morphology-specific normaliza-
tion: omitted attributes are assigned defaults, unsupported
arguments are discarded, and primitives that cannot be parsed
are excluded. We use Gemini 2.5 Pro through the Gemini
API and retain one generation per cue–morphology pair.
For the five reported morphologies, all 150 programs in the
50-cue generation set passed the parser, corresponding to a
format-failure rate of 0/150.
b) Motion compilation and rendering.: The compiler
deterministically grounds each symbolic program into a
morphology-conditioned trajectory. For articulated robots,
a requested pose first filters the feasible pose set by
categorical attributes such as body-relative direction, grip-
per orientation, palm direction, and elbow configuration. A
movement is compiled relative to the selected pose. At
the current configuration, the compiler computes the end-
effector Jacobian and selects the degree of freedom whose
motion is most strongly aligned with the requested Cartesian
axis, while treating the specified anatomical group—such as
the shoulder, elbow, or wrist—as a soft preference. It then
applies the authored angular displacement and interpolates
the resulting joint-space trajectory; signed ranges and rep
values produce oscillatory or repeated motions. A path is
compiled as an inverse-kinematic end-effector trajectory for
manipulators or as planar translation and yaw for mobile
bases.
Timing is computed uniformly from the authored spd,
rep, and hold parameters. The speed is ranged in
[0.25, 4.0]. A joint-motion beat with r repetitions has nom-

spd whereas a path of total length

B. Metrics
1) Human Recognition: We use a hard-negative 4-
Alternative Forced-Choice task (4AFC; 25% chance) to test
whether an observer can recover the intended cue from
a silent motion clip. We select the three nearest cues in
the Motion-Program representation using Jaccard similarity.
These alternatives are fixed across participants, while their
presentation order is randomized. Participants watch silent
clips without a viewing-time and replay limit before selection
considering the cognitive load. Ten participants each evaluate
50 unique trials, with no trial repeated across participants.

strategies introduced in §III-C according to how the cue-
relevant carrier and attributes change across morphologies.
• Preserved: The same or anatomically corresponding
carrier is available, and its recognition-relevant Move-

tained.
• Carrier-transferred: The original carrier is unavail-

an appropriate realization of the intended cue. If the

movement disappears altogether, we label it a Remate-
rialization Error.
VI. RESULTS
A. Human-like Robots
a) Human recognition: The 4AFC study shows a clear
gap between human- and LLM-authored programs. Human-
authored programs are recognized at 96.7% on G1 and 93.3%
on TIAGo, whereas Gemini-authored programs reach only
63.3% and 60.0%, respectively. Performance changes little
between G1 and TIAGo within each authoring condition.

b) Attribute-level errors: Table ?? localizes this gap at
the attribute level. For location, Gemini’s mean z error is 11.2

along x and y (18.4/19.2 on G1 and 14.7/19.8 on TIAGo).
Categorical attributes show a different pattern. Reach agree-
ment is 47.2% and 49.1%, well above its 1/6 chance level,
indicating that the model often selects a broadly appropriate
direction. Palm orientation is much weaker: Gemini reaches
48.6% on G1 and 52.8% on TIAGo, compared with chance
levels of 25% and 50%, respectively. This suggests that
palm configuration is not reliably preserved, particularly
when it is not strongly constrained by the cue. Movement
is more accurately captured overall, with axis agreement
of 58.0% and 63.2% and sign agreement of 56.8% and
72.2%. However, the remaining errors are often semantically
consequential: cues such as air quotes can activate the
appropriate carrier and repeated motion while moving along
an incorrect axis.
c) Qualitative patterns.: The LLM’s prior knowledge
is most useful for cues with a conventional iconic form.
For depict flying bird, it spreads both arms, turns the
palms down, and reproduces the GT’s four z-axis flaps; for
depict big bowl, it places both palms up and traces a bi-
lateral yz arc. It also selects the expected carrier without con-
fusion for movement-dominant cues: nod yes uses head tilt,
shake head no uses head pan, and gaze shift avoid
keeps the GT’s head-pan carrier. The failures instead reveal
semantic completion without geometric grounding: because
generation is single-pass and does not inspect the rendered
body, the model often captures the verbal concept but assigns
attributes that do not realize it physically. In point self,
for example, the GT points back toward the torso, whereas
the LLM sets dir=front; in cover yawn sleepy, it
moves a hand toward the face but replaces the GT’s upward,
horizontal hand with an inward, vertical palm. Likewise,
its bowl has the correct palm-up arc but reverses the GT’s
outward reach to inward. Thus, semantic knowledge reliably
proposes what should move and the rough iconic stroke,
while embodied orientation remains the principal grounding
failure.
B. Non-anthropomorphic Robots
a) Human recognition: Figure 4 shows that unfamiliar
morphology does not itself prevent communication. Human-
authored programs remain recognizable on IIWA (90.0%),
Stretch (93.3%), and Roomba (90.0%), despite the progres-
sive loss of hands, a head, and articulated limbs. LLM-
authored programs instead decline from 60.0% to 56.7% and
46.7%, widening the human–LLM gap from 30.0 to 43.3
percentage points.
b) Cue-generation richness.: In Table ??, program
length counts components such as pose, move, path
while Attributes/program sums specified attributes across
components in a program. Length alone, however, can-
not distinguish geometrically different motions. We there-
fore introduce Types/Cue: the number of distinct dynamic
families—oscillation, line, arc, and one-way joint motion.
IIWA is similar under both authoring methods, but with
Stretch, Human-authored programs are longer (3.9 vs. 2.8),
contain more attributes (15.3 vs. 10.7), and combine more
motion families (1.33 vs. 1.10). On Roomba, the LLM
is longer (3.2 vs. 2.8) yet uses fewer attributes (9.1 vs.
11.2) and fewer families (1.20 vs. 1.57). Thus, the LLM
often adds steps without adding a comparably rich motion
vocabulary, whereas humans vary geometry, timing, and
carrier combinations according to the cue.
c) Morphological collapse on Roomba.: This lack of
compositional diversity helps explain why Roomba clips
are especially confusable with their hard negatives. Across
the complete 50-cue LLM set, 34/50 programs (68.0%)
use fore–aft translation. Of those, 21/34 (61.8%) contain
no additional rotation, arc, or lateral motion. Once the
body has collapsed to a single mobile base, such programs
map semantically different cues—including point you,
salute, highfive invite, and stretch arms up—
to variants of approach, retreat, and hold, reducing their
contrast against nearby answer choices. Human authors in-
stead use the base’s orientation and trajectory as surrogate
anatomy. In beckon come here, they retreat, rotate 90◦
so the elongated body reads like an extended carrier, and
then oscillate its heading. These constructions do not restore
the missing limbs, but they approximate the intended spatial
relation with a richer sequence of planar evidence, consistent
with the higher Human Types/Cue value.

C. New Morphologies
We use synthetic morphologies to ask two questions: (1)
Can recognition-relevant attributes be defined for complex
bodies far from human? and (2) Can these attributes be
composed into Motion Programs that convey intended mean-
ings through such unfamiliar bodies to LLM? Five familiar
platforms may benefit from memorized body-specific map-
pings. We therefore introduce three synthetic morphologies
as an out-of-distribution probe that removes this shortcut.
As shown in Figure 5, TrilegCrown has no head, hands, or
stable front; SpineCrab replaces conventional limbs with a
canted spine and two unequal pincers; and PaddleClaw, the
least unfamiliar of the three, retains a head but replaces the
upper body with a paddle and a claw.
a) Unfamiliar parts become communicative carriers:
The programs do not route every cue through a single
surrogate limb. On TrilegCrown, 20 of the 30 cues use
the front leg, 17 the base, and 9 and 7 the two other
legs; on SpineCrab, 22 use the long pincer, 12 the short
pincer, and 11 the spine. Carrier choice follows the cue:
depict flying bird oscillates TrilegCrown’s two rear
legs, shake head no twists SpineCrab’s spine in place,
and tilt head curious turns SpineCrab aside with its
antenna pointed forward.
b) The same meaning is rematerialized, not only reas-
signed: For 15 of the 30 cues, the motion structure itself—
static pose, joint oscillation, or traced path—differs across
the three bodies, so the change goes beyond swapping a car-
rier name. nod yes is a head tilt on PaddleClaw and a spine
pitch on SpineCrab, but an in-place yaw rock of the base on
TrilegCrown. beckon come here is a repeated claw or
pincer-tip oscillation on the two branched bodies, whereas
TrilegCrown raises its front leg, curls the knee three times,
and advances toward the viewer. depict big bowl traces
a vertical end-effector arc on PaddleClaw and SpineCrab
but a 180◦
floor arc of the whole base on TrilegCrown,
changing carrier, plane, and scale together. These cases are
the morphology-conditioned rematerialization of Sec. III-C,
not anatomical substitution.
c) Where the probe breaks: The same programs expose
three failures. Contrast collapse: as channels disappear, dis-
tinct cues share one program. On TrilegCrown, point you,
salute, and flex bicep all reduce to the same presented
front leg, and nod yes and shake head no both become
base yaw oscillations that differ only in amplitude (±12◦
vs.
±25◦
), erasing the vertical–lateral axis contrast that defines
them (Sec. III-B). On SpineCrab, cover yawn sleepy
and facepalm are identical, and on PaddleClaw five cues
are static claw poses distinguished only by a direction
token. Human-form leakage: six bilateral cues, including
arms open welcome, cross arms defensive, and
depict flying bird, set SpineCrab’s short pincer out
or in, a configuration its schema does not expose, in effect
reconstructing a human pair of arms; TrilegCrown’s front leg
likewise stands in for a hand across pointing, saluting, and
flexing.
VII. CONCLUSION
Humans are highly sensitive to agency and intent in
motion, attributing meaning to the movements of animals,
infants, animated characters, and even simple shapes. Robots
enter the same perceptual process: whether a motion is

produced for a functional purpose or deliberately designed
as a gesture, once observed, it becomes evidence from
which people form an interpretation. The relevant question is
therefore not simply whether a robot can move expressively,
but whether its particular body preserves the evidence needed
for an intended meaning. Orientation, body-relative location,
and movement provide compact handles for posing and di-
agnosing that question, complemented by timing, repetition,
scale, coordination, and context. Motion Programs expose
these handles to both people and LLMs and compile them
through morphology-specific renderers.
The results reveal two distinct limitations. On human-like
bodies, all of the necessary carriers are available, yet LLM-
authored programs frequently misplace and misorient them.
On non-humanoid bodies, generation draws from a more re-
stricted repertoire: some cues transfer or rematerialize, while
others become ambiguous because their defining evidence
is lost. These findings leave two concrete problems for the
community. First, how can we lower the authoring barrier
so that every robot can be given an editable repertoire of
recognizable gestures, even when humans must define the
semantics? Second, how can models learn to reason about
a new morphology well enough to invent the right carrier
and motion, rather than merely imitate the surface form of a
human gesture? Answering the second question will require
evaluation on meaning recognition across genuinely different
bodies, not only realism on a familiar human skeleton.

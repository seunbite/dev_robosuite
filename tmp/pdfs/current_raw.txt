What Makes a Robot Gesture Recognizable?
Gesture Generation Across Robot Morphologies
Anonymous Authors
Abstract— It is well established that humans’ ability to read
meaning from movement is not tied to the actor’s human body.
Likewise, we can imagine communicative behaviors no longer
being confined to human or humanoid gestures, but instead
emerging as forms of language shaped by different bodies.
We raise cross-morphology gesture generation as a research
question: what aspects of a gesture must be preserved, trans-
ferred, or reformulated for its meaning to remain recognizable
across embodiments? We posit three general attributes for
gesture recognition—orientation, location, and movement—and
introduce Symbolic Motion Programs (Motion Programs), an
executable symbolic representation that makes these attributes
explicit across different robot morphologies. We evaluate 50
gesture cues across eight morphologies and observe two distinct
challenges across embodiment and author types. On human-like
robots, LLM-authored gestures mis-specify recognition-critical
details such as orientation, resulting in a consistent human–AI
gap (Unitree G1 61.1% vs. 46.7%). On non-anthropomorphic
robots, the challenge shifts to reconstructing communicative
evidence through different physical carriers; however, LLMs of-
ten transfer familiar motion patterns onto different body parts
(retargeting), rather than reformulating the gesture through
the motion channels available in the new body (rematerializa-
tion). Finally, we test three novel synthetic morphologies and
show that LLMs can recruit unfamiliar body parts such as
asymmetric arms or collar in neck as communicative carriers,
demonstrating the potential for a general tool that can support
continuous and open-ended variation in robot morphology.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humans possess an innate and responsive sensor for
reading underlying meaning from the movement of others.
This capacity extends even to non-human actors: research
in psychology and neuroscience has shown that people infer
animate structure from sparse moving points [1], goals from
observed actions [2], and social interaction from the motion
of simple geometric shapes [3]. More broadly, communi-
cation through nonverbal bodily signals is pervasive across
animal interaction [4], and some bodily motion can become
a conventionalized signal for conveying social meaning [5].
We posit that robot motion is no exception; rather, this is
a central problem for human–robot interaction: how humans
read semantics from robot motion and how robots correctly
deliver the intended meaning across increasingly diverse
robot morphologies.
Gesture generation provides a particularly useful setting
in which to study this problem because discrete gestures
often are binded with specific body parts (e.g., nod - head).
Communicating a specific discrete meaning such as beckon
come here, pat stomach, or stop palm out is different from

orientation, location, and movement—as the commonly im-

As Figure 1 illustrates, realizing the same gesture on
human-like and non-anthropomorphic bodies poses different
problems. For human-like bodies, deviations in particular
properties such as hand orientation and movement direc-
tion can substantially alter interpretation. In contrast, non-
anthropomorphic robots have simplified or altered physical
carriers for conveying meaning, and therefore often require
rematerialization rather than simple retargeting. By retar-
geting, we mean simply planting the current movement in
humnaoid body onto a different carrier; by rematerializa-
tion, it reformulates how that communicative evidence is
physically realized through the parts and motion channels
available in a new body. For example, a Roomba may depict
flying bird through a bird-like movement trajectory
rather than through arm movements.
We introduce Symbolic Motion Programs (Motion Pro-
grams) as an executable representation for studying this
cross-embodiment problem and evaluate them across eight
different robot bodies and 50 different cues. A Motion
Program is a general grammar that humans and LLMs can
author, inspect, and edit, providing a practical starting point
for cross-morphology gesture generation. Across human-like
robots, we find a clear human-AI gap in gesture generation
associated with errors in recognition-relevant attributes (e.g.,
movement direction in ‘air quote’). On non-anthropomorphic
robots, LLM-authored programs show a clear pattern of
‘retargeting’ rather than ‘rematerialization’, with 34/50 pro-
grams (68.0%) using back-and-forth oscillation to express
the cue. Finally, we test the same problem on synthetic mor-
phologies without relying on familiar robot designs: LLMs
use unfamiliar body parts to construct gesture realizations,
but distinct cues can also collapse into similar motions in
non-anthropomorphic bodies.

2) A morphology-agnostic generation framework:

3) A diagnosis of present limits: humanoid experiments

II. RELATED WORK
A. Expressive Robot Motion Generation
We posit human-like robot body not a prerequisite to
express concrete mind state in daily life. Early work on
communicative robot motion authored robot-specific ges-
tures through multimodal behavior languages [6], [7], [8] or
designed learning to reproduce human motion from paired
speech and motion data [9], [10], [11] using humanoid body.
More recent work has shown that communicative motion
on a human-like body planting timing, energy, and Laban-
derived motion qualities to shape confidence, affect, and
social state [12], [13], [14]. These studies establish that
non-humanoid motion can be socially legible, but typically
focus on latent affective dimensions or platform-specific
repertoires. On the other hand, we study how a concrete
communicative meaning is realized as the available body
parts and motion channels change across morphologies.
B. Motion Generation with Large Language Models (LLMs)
Language models generate robot motion at several levels:
they can emit code that invokes skills [15], construct spatial
objectives or value maps that guide control [16], select or pa-
rameterize learned motion primitives [17], and generate or re-
trieve expressive animations from a prompted repertoire [18].
Across these systems, reliability is sought through structured
APIs, few-shot demonstrations, executable constraints, ob-
jective construction, or verification and correction around

a predefined skill library. For gesture, however, physical

III. GESTURE GENERATION FRAMEWORK
A. Gesture: Meaning and Action Mapping
Gesture generation maps a meaning space G (e.g., nodding
yes, stopping, beckoning, or depicting a flying bird) to
an action space A(M) shaped by robot morphology M.
For example, a Roomba realizes motion primarily through
changes in x, y, and θ, whereas a humanoid has many
articulated carriers. Within each action space, a meaning
can admit infinitely many continuous realizations, but not
all variations contribute equally to recognition. We suggest
that some attributes are recognition-critical: changing them
can weaken the evidence for the intended cue G and shift
interpretation toward plausible alternatives G′
. For example,
reversing the palm orientation in a stop gesture can make
the motion resemble ‘looking into a hand mirror’.
B. Recognition-Critical Attributes
In Figure 2, we identify three important attributes that
commonly contribute to gesture recognition, with their rela-
tive importance varying across gesture cues. This formulation
follows the concept of family resemblance [19], in which cat-
egory members share overlapping features without requiring
a single set of necessary and sufficient conditions, making
some members more typical than others (e.g., typ(robin |
bird) > typ(penguin | bird)).
a) Orientation (O): Orientation describes where a
recognition-relevant carrier—such as the head, hand, foot,
or torso—is directed. Although orientation is physically
continuous, we argue that many communicative distinctions
can be captured by a small set of discrete relations: front,
back, in, out, up, and down. For example, point you
should orient its pointing carrier toward the addressee in
front; stop palm out presents the palm toward front while
directing the fingertips up.
b) Location (L): Location describes where the relevant
carrier lies relative to the body or environment. Location is
naturally continuous and can require different spatial reso-
lutions across cues, for example, in rub eye an incorrect
z coordinate can move the end effector from the eye to
the forehead or nose, changing the intended interpretation.
Therefore, continuous (x, y, z) locations are needed to spec-
ify precise cue realization.
c) Movement (M): Movement describes how a carrier
changes over time and captures temporal structure that
cannot be specified by pose alone. Movement differs in
axis/sign (movement direction), extent, speed, and repetition.
Among these, axis and sign often provide particularly strong
recognition evidence. A nod requires repeated motion along
the vertical (z) axis, whereas a head shake requires lateral
(y) oscillation. Similarly, raise fists requires upward
(z+) movement rather than downward (z−) movement.

d) Realization strategy: Transferring a gesture to a
different morphology changes which carriers and attributes
are physically available. Movement structure is often com-
paratively portable: an axis, direction, or repetition pattern
can be reconstructed with other body parts in the changed
morphology. Orientation may instead need to migrate to
another carrier when the original one does not exist, while
location is particularly morphology-dependent because body-
relative landmarks and reachable regions change with em-
bodiment.
Retargeting preserves the recognition-relevant structure
of a gesture while mapping it onto the target morphology.
The original carrier may remain available, as in a human
head nod → TIAGo head nod, or the same attributes may
be transferred to an anatomically different carrier, as in a
vertical head oscillation → vertical single-arm oscillation.
In both cases, the carrier may change, but the movement
that characterize the gesture remain largely preserved. Rema-
terialization reformulates how the gesture’s communicative
evidence is physically realized when the target morphology
does not support a direct mapping. Rather than transferring
the same motion to another carrier, it may change the
movement, orientation, spatial relations, or their combination
to express the cue through the available body. For example,
bilateral arm flapping can be rematerialized as a bird-like
trajectory traced by a planar mobile robot. Rematerialization
may involve emphasizing an available attribute, combining
multiple attributes, or replacing an unavailable realization
with a different motion that preserves cue-relevant informa-
tion.
IV. SYMBOLIC MOTION PROGRAMS
We introduce Symbolic Motion Programs (Motion Pro-
grams), a compact executable representation of the evidence
that makes a gesture recognizable. As illustrated in Fig. 3,
a program composes three typed primitives: pose speci-
fies a carrier’s orientation and location; movement applies
a joint-level change to an articulated carrier; and path
traces a Cartesian trajectory with an end effector or mobile
base. For example, the human-authored G1 program for
beckon come here is
pose(dir=front, grip=vert|palmin,
z=80, x=60) + move(wrist, x, [-35,+35],
rep=3, spd=3.5, hold=0.1)
Here, pose selects the right hand as the carrier, directs the
arm toward the observer, turns the palm inward, and places
the hand high and forward in the workspace. The following
movement oscillates the wrist through a signed range of

structure of the action and the morphology-specific compiler

A. Pre-authoring Stage: Design by Human Authors
1) Morphology Schema (YAML): To provide both human
and LLM authors with explicit information about each
robot’s available motion channels, we encode the morphol-
ogy as a text, YAML schema. The schema specifies the
meaning, value range (discrete or continuous), default value,
and examples for each available attribute. The grammar
of movement and path remains largely shared across
embodiments, using attributes such as joint, axis, angle,
speed, repetition, hold time, and arc and line parameters.
In contrast, pose attributes are more morphology-specific
and act as filters over the robot’s available pose set to get
the appropriate configuration.
2) Few-Shot Examples: Human authors manually de-
sign the same five cue—raising hand greeting, wave hi,
bow greeting, blow kiss, and circle temple crazy—for each
morphology. These cues do not overlap with the 50 cues
used in our evaluation. Given only these examples and the
morphology’s YAML, few shot generation can be automated
with an LLM authoring and human inspection.
3) Feasible Pose Sets: We additionally construct fea-
sible pose sets for G1 and TIAGo. Both have human-
like upper bodies, their joint ranges exceed typical hu-
man articulation; therefore physically valid but unnaturally
folded pose hampers gesture recognizability. We found this
difficult to filter reliably with a VLM, and instead use a
deterministic procedure. We enumerate joint configurations

B. Automated Stage: LLM Authoring and Simulation
a) Program authoring.: For each cue–morphology pair,
the LLM receives the cue name, YAML description of the
target morphology, and five shots rather than the raw XML.
The model then produces a single Motion Program. A deter-
ministic parser converts the generated text into an executable
configuration and performs morphology-specific normaliza-
tion: omitted attributes are assigned defaults, unsupported
arguments are discarded, and primitives that cannot be parsed
are excluded. We use Gemini 2.5 Pro through the Gemini
API and retain one generation per cue–morphology pair.
For the five reported morphologies, all 150 programs in the
50-cue generation set passed the parser, corresponding to a
format-failure rate of 0/150.
b) Motion compilation and rendering.: The compiler
deterministically grounds each symbolic program into a
morphology-conditioned trajectory. For articulated robots,
a requested pose first filters the feasible pose set by
categorical attributes such as body-relative direction, grip-
per orientation, palm direction, and elbow configuration. A
movement is compiled relative to the selected pose. At
the current configuration, the compiler computes the end-
effector Jacobian and selects the degree of freedom whose
motion is most strongly aligned with the requested Cartesian
axis, while treating the specified anatomical group—such as
the shoulder, elbow, or wrist—as a soft preference. It then
applies the authored angular displacement and interpolates
the resulting joint-space trajectory; signed ranges and rep
values produce oscillatory or repeated motions. A path is
compiled as an inverse-kinematic end-effector trajectory for
manipulators or as planar translation and yaw for mobile
bases.
Timing is computed uniformly from the authored spd,
rep, and hold parameters. The speed is ranged in
[0.25, 4.0]. A joint-motion beat with r repetitions has nom-

B. Metrics
1) Human Recognition: We use a hard-negative 4-
Alternative Forced-Choice task (4AFC; 25% chance) to test
whether an observer can recover the intended cue from
a silent motion clip. We select the three nearest cues in
the Motion-Program representation using Jaccard similarity.
These alternatives are fixed across participants, while their
presentation order is randomized. Participants watch silent
clips without a viewing-time and replay limit before selection
considering the cognitive load. Ten participants each evaluate
50 unique trials, with no trial repeated across participants.

generated gesture succeeds or fails, we additionally compare
its Location, Orientation, and Movement attributes against
the corresponding human-authored ground truth (GT).

the relevant carrier (end effector), normalized to the
robot’s reachable range (e.g., z ∈ [0, 100], where 0 and
100 denote the lowest and highest reachable positions).
We report the mean absolute difference; thus, a value
of 0 indicates the same location as the GT.

VI. RESULTS
A. Human-like Robots
a) Human recognition: Figure 4 first shows that both
human- and LLM-authored programs produce recognizable
gestures across multiple robot embodiments. All condi-
tions except LLM-authored Roomba remain above the 25%
random-choice baseline, even though the alternatives in
our 4AFC task are hard negatives selected to be con-
fusable with the target (e.g., hands up surrender and
stretch arms up). The degree of recognition differs
across authors and morphologies. On the two human-like
robots, human-authored programs reach 61.1% on G1 and
66.7% on TIAGo, compared with 46.7% and 53.3% for
Gemini-authored programs. Human-authored recognition is
also highest on these two robots and decreases on the less
anthropomorphic bodies, reaching 50.0% on IIWA, 44.4%
on Stretch, and 38.9% on Roomba. Thus, while recogniz-
able gestures can be realized across substantially different
embodiments, the human-like bodies provide the strongest
recognition, and a consistent human–LLM gap remains on
G1 and TIAGo.
b) Carrier Selection and Iconicity: The LLM generally
identifies which body part should carry a gesture, espe-
cially when a familiar anatomical correspondence is avail-
able. nod yes uses head tilt, shake head no uses head
pan. This grounding is particularly effective for cues with
a conventional iconic form. For depict flying bird,
the LLM spreads both arms, turns the palms down,
and reproduces the reference’s four z-axis flaps; for
depict big bowl, it places both palms up and traces a
bilateral yz arc.
c) Attribute-level errors: Although the LLM generally
identifies plausible carriers and motions, it is less reliable
in specifying their recognition-critical attributes. Table II
localizes these errors. For location, Gemini’s mean z error
is 11.2 body-% on G1 and 8.7 on TIAGo, while errors are
larger along x and y (18.4/19.2 on G1 and 14.7/19.8 on
TIAGo). Reach agreement is 47.2% and 49.1%, above its
1/6 chance level, indicating that the model frequently selects
the same broad direction as the human-authored reference.
Palm orientation is less consistent: agreement reaches 48.6%
on G1 and 52.8% on TIAGo, compared with chance levels of
25% and 50%. Movement is preserved more frequently, with
axis agreement of 58.0% and 63.2% and sign agreement of
56.8% and 72.2%. The larger Gemini model generally shows
lower error and higher agreement than 32B except several
cases of Reach and Sign agreement.
d) Semantics Mislead Direction: The failures reveal
a recurring pattern: the LLM produces poses that are se-
mantically plausible from the cue description, but do not
necessarily correspond to how the gesture should physically
appear. For cover yawn sleepy, for example, the LLM
sets the hand direction to front, plausibly placing the hand
toward the front of the body. However, for the hand to cover
the face, its tip should instead point upward or inward. A
similar error occurs in pat stomach: the LLM directs the
hand backward, toward the stomach, apparently conflating
the target of the gesture with the orientation of the hand
itself. The hand should instead remain oriented inward or
upward while being positioned near the stomach. These cases
suggest that the LLM captures the semantic relation between

C. New Morphologies
Robot morphology is an open-ended, continuous design
space. We therefore use three synthetic bodies to ask two
questions: (1) can an unfamiliar body be integrated into
the same Motion Program grammar and compiler, and (2)
will an LLM recruit unfamiliar, non-anthropomorphic parts
as morphology-aware communicative carriers? As shown in
Fig. 5, PaddleClaw replaces matched hands with an asym-
metric paddle and claw; TrilegCrown has three articulated
radial legs but no head or arms; and SpineCrab combines a
canted spine with unequal pincers and small auxiliary parts
in torso.
a) Attribute design.: PaddleClaw provides an articu-
lated head and trunk, independent paddle and claw branches
as they are asymmetric, a claw grip, paddle orientation,
and a satellite-ring spinner. TrilegCrown provides a planar
base, three independent hip–knee chains, and a dorsal crown
spinner. SpineCrab provides a mobile base, articulated spine,
distinct long and short pincers, an antenna, flaps, and an in-
dependently rotating collar. These schemas admit unfamiliar
carriers within Motion Program grammer.
b) Unfamiliar parts become communicative carriers:
The LLM exploits alien-like channels to express gestures

depict flying bird; its crown oscillates to express

SpineCrab assigns the antenna to tilt head curious,

that of familiar body parts—for example, PaddleClaw’s
satellite-ring spinner is never selected in the 50-cue set—we
hypothesize that the use of these unfamiliar parts is sensitive
to, and could be increased by, the distribution of the
few-shot examples.
c) Rematerializing meaning.: For 30 of the 50 cues,
the coarse motion structure—static pose, joint oscillation,
or traced path—differs across the three bodies, so adapta-
tion goes beyond replacing one carrier name with another.
nod yes is a head tilt on PaddleClaw and a spine pitch

base. beckon come here is a repeated claw or pincer-tip

substitutions.
d) Grounding failures.: Novel morphologies reveal
similar issues to those observed with non-anthropomorphic
bodies, such as contrast collapse: as channels disappear,
distinct cues can collapse into the same program. On Tri-
legCrown, point you, salute, and flex bicep all
reduce to presenting the same front leg, while nod yes

). We also
observe minor imperfections in otherwise plausible Motion
Programs. For example, visor search is canonically
expressed by raising a hand to eye level, but the motion
program of Spinecrab leaves the pincers still.
VII. CONCLUSION
Humans are highly sensitive to agency and intent in
motion, attributing meaning to the movements of animals,
infants, animated characters, and even simple shapes. Robots
enter the same perceptual process: whether a motion is
produced for a functional purpose or deliberately designed
as a gesture, once observed, it becomes evidence from
which people form an interpretation. The relevant question is
therefore not simply whether a robot can move expressively,
but whether its particular body preserves the evidence needed
for an intended meaning. Orientation, location, and move-
ment provide compact handles for posing and diagnosing
that question, complemented by timing, repetition, scale,
coordination, and context. Motion Programs expose these
handles to both people and LLMs and compile them through
morphology-specific renderers.
The results reveal two distinct limitations. On human-like
bodies, all of the necessary carriers are available, yet LLM-
authored programs frequently misplace and misorient them.
On non-humanoid bodies, generation draws from a more re-
stricted repertoire: some cues transfer or rematerialize, while
others become ambiguous because their defining evidence
is lost. These findings leave two concrete problems for the
community. First, how can we lower the authoring barrier
so that every robot can be given an editable repertoire of
recognizable gestures, even when humans must define the
semantics? Second, how can models learn to reason about
a new morphology well enough to invent the right carrier
and motion, rather than merely imitate the surface form of a
human gesture? Answering the second question will require
evaluation on meaning recognition across genuinely different
bodies, not only realism on a familiar human skeleton.
